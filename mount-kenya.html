Mount Kenya
Conquer the Peak

A UNESCO World Heritage site and Africa's second-highest peak — an unforgettable adventure through alpine forests and glaciers.

Overview

Africa's Second-Highest Peak

Mount Kenya is Africa's second-highest mountain at 5,199 meters (17,057 feet), surpassed only by Kilimanjaro. This ancient extinct volcano is a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of Kenya's most spectacular natural wonders. The mountain is a dormant stratovolcano that last erupted approximately 2.6 million years ago, with its jagged peaks and glaciers carved by millennia of glacial activity.

Mount Kenya's slopes feature distinct ecological zones — from lush montane forest at lower elevations through bamboo and heath zones to the stark alpine desert and glaciers at the summit. The mountain is a critical water tower for Kenya, feeding the Tana River system that provides hydroelectric power and irrigation to much of the country.

Trekking Routes

Paths to the Summit

Mount Kenya offers several established trekking routes, each with its own character and challenges. All routes require a good level of fitness and proper acclimatization.

  • Sirimon Route — The most popular and recommended route. Approaches from the northwest with gentle gradients and excellent acclimatization. Stunning views and good hut accommodation. Usually takes 5-7 days.
  • Chogoria Route — Often considered the most scenic approach. Features breathtaking views of the Gorges Valley, Hall Tarns, and the Temple. Begins from the east side. Combines with Sirimon for a traverse.
  • Naro Moru Route — The fastest but most challenging route. A steep climb through the Vertical Bog and up to the summit. Popular for experienced trekkers seeking a quicker ascent.
Wildlife

Life on the Mountain

Mount Kenya's forests are home to a variety of wildlife including elephants, buffaloes, leopards, and the elusive bongo antelope. The lower slopes host Sykes monkeys, colobus monkeys, and large forest hogs. Birdlife is abundant with over 130 bird species recorded including the stunning Mount Kenya ibis, sunbirds, and eagles.

As you ascend above the tree line, wildlife becomes scarce but the unique giant groundsel and lobelia plants create an otherworldly landscape. The alpine zone features unique flora adapted to extreme temperature swings, with days above 30°C and nights below freezing.

Climbing Tips

Prepare for the Summit

Climbing Mount Kenya requires proper preparation. The most important factors are acclimatization, proper gear, and fitness. Most climbers take 5-7 days for a summit attempt. Essential gear includes warm sleeping bags rated to -15°C, waterproof boots, trekking poles, and multiple clothing layers. Hiring a licensed guide and porters is mandatory and supports local employment.

Altitude sickness is a real risk above 3,500 meters. The "pole pole" (slowly slowly) approach to pacing, combined with proper hydration and the medication Diamox, helps mitigate symptoms. Acclimatization days should never be skipped, regardless of fitness level.

Best Time to Climb

When to Go

Dry Seasons (January-February & July-October): The best times for climbing. Clear skies, minimal rainfall, and relatively warm temperatures on the slopes. Summit views are often spectacular during these months.

Wet Seasons (March-June & November-December): The mountain receives heavy rainfall, trails become muddy, and summit success rates are lower. However, the scenery is lush and there are far fewer climbers on the routes.

For the best weather windows and highest summit success rates, plan your climb during the dry seasons.

Conservation

Protecting the Peak

Mount Kenya National Park is a UNESCO World Heritage site and a vital conservation area. The park is managed by the Kenya Wildlife Service with support from community conservancies. Mount Kenya's glaciers have shrunk by more than 80% since the early 1900s due to climate change, making conservation efforts more urgent than ever. Visitors are asked to follow Leave No Trace principles and use designated campsites.
